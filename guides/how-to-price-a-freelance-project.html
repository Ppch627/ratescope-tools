Project pricing

How to price a freelance project

A practical seven-step process for moving from an unclear client request to a quote that is clear, useful, and still profitable after the work is delivered.

Updated September 18, 2026 · 9 minute read

This guide is general business information, not legal, tax, or accounting advice. Contract and payment requirements vary by jurisdiction and client type.

1. Define the outcome before estimating the work

A client rarely buys “a website,” “some copy,” or “a logo.” They buy a change in their business: more qualified leads, a launch that happens on time, a clearer message, or a process that no longer depends on one person. Before discussing hours, write one sentence that describes the result.

For example: “The client will have a five-page website that can accept inquiries, with approved copy, mobile layouts, analytics, and a short training video.” That sentence is not a full contract, but it gives you something concrete to estimate.

Ask questions that reveal constraints:

  • Who approves the final work, and how many other stakeholders can change it?
  • What must be provided by the client, and by when?
  • What does success look like, and how will both sides know it happened?
  • What is the deadline, and what happens if the deadline is unrealistic?
  • Are accessibility, security, legal review, translation, or integrations required?

If the answers are unclear, your estimate is not unclear because you are bad at estimating. It is unclear because the input is incomplete. Price the clarification as a separate discovery phase or include a larger uncertainty buffer.

2. List every deliverable and workflow step

Break the project into activities rather than thinking about the final artifact. A small deliverable may require discovery, research, setup, drafts, review, revision, quality assurance, file preparation, handover, and follow-up.

A useful estimate has four columns:

  1. Activity: what you will do.
  2. Hours: your best realistic estimate.
  3. Rate or cost: your internal hourly cost or an external expense.
  4. Dependency: what must happen before this work can start.

Do not estimate only the first draft. The last 10% often contains the most coordination, approval, and rework. If three people must approve the work, the project has more review capacity than a one-person project even when the output is the same.

3. Use a real internal hourly rate

Use your sustainable hourly rate as the cost of your time even when the client buys a fixed outcome. A rate built from income, business costs, billable capacity, and time off prevents the quote from quietly becoming a donation.

For a quick example, suppose a project needs 40 production hours, 6 revision hours, and 5 admin hours. At an internal rate of $95, the labor estimate is $4,845 before risk, tax, or external costs. If the project takes 70 hours because the brief changes, a $4,845 fixed price is no longer the same economic deal.

Use the project quote calculator to see the effect of revisions, admin, risk, tax, and deposit. Then compare the client-facing price with the market range and the value of the outcome. A low price is not automatically competitive; it may simply attract clients with the most demanding process.

4. Add uncertainty as a visible business decision

Risk buffers are not padding for the sake of a bigger invoice. They compensate you for uncertainty that the client controls or benefits from: vague feedback, delayed assets, new stakeholders, changing priorities, or technology you cannot fully test in advance.

Ask what would make the estimate most wrong. If a single unknown could add a week of work, either:

  • increase the price to absorb the uncertainty,
  • add a discovery phase to reduce it,
  • define a change order for the uncertain part, or
  • decline the project if the risk cannot be priced fairly.

Never hide the risk buffer as a secret number. You do not need to reveal every line item, but the quote should explain that the price assumes a defined scope, timeline, and approval process.

5. Decide what is included and what costs extra

Scope is the boundary that makes a fixed price possible. Write down included quantities and formats. For example: two concept directions, two rounds of revisions, one kickoff meeting, one review meeting, delivery in two formats, and one week of post-launch support.

Then define what is out of scope. Common extras include additional concepts, expanded pages, new audiences, rush delivery, weekend support, additional languages, content migration, training beyond the agreed session, and changes after approval.

A change order does not need to be hostile. It can be a short email: “This request adds two new page templates and a second approval round. It changes the estimate by $X and moves delivery by Y days. Shall I proceed on that basis?”

6. Price payment terms, not just the total

Cash flow is part of profitability. A $10,000 project paid 90 days after completion can damage your business more than a $8,000 project paid in milestones. Decide the deposit and milestones before work begins.

A simple structure for a medium project might be:

  • 30% deposit to reserve the schedule and begin discovery.
  • 30% after approval of the core direction or first milestone.
  • 40% before final files, transfer, or publication, subject to the contract.

State the currency, tax treatment, payment method, due date, late-fee terms, and who pays transaction costs. If your contract allows late fees, make sure the rate and notice process comply with applicable law.

7. Present a decision, not a defenseless number

A quote lands better when it connects the price to the outcome, scope, schedule, and responsibilities. Lead with the result and the boundary, then give the investment. Avoid apologetic language such as “I know this is a little high, but…” and avoid a discount before the client has even asked.

A concise proposal can contain:

  1. The outcome and what changes for the client.
  2. Deliverables, included revision rounds, and exclusions.
  3. Timeline and client dependencies.
  4. Price, taxes, deposit, and milestone schedule.
  5. Acceptance process and change-order rule.

Give the prospect a clear next step and an expiration date if your capacity is limited. Then stop negotiating against yourself. If the client asks for a lower number, reduce scope or offer a smaller phase with a clear outcome.

A quick worked example

Assume 40 production hours, 6 revision hours, and 5 admin hours at an internal rate of $95. The labor estimate is $4,845. Add a 15% scope-risk buffer of $727, bringing the subtotal to $5,572. If a 10% tax or reserve applies, add $557. The client-facing total is about $6,129. A 25% deposit is about $1,532.

That does not prove the market will pay $6,129. It proves the price covers the entered business assumptions. If the market will not pay it, change the scope, use a less expensive delivery method, target a different buyer, or produce a smaller first phase. Do not reduce the number while pretending the same cost structure still works.

Check the quote before you send it

Run the numbers, reread the scope from the client's perspective, and ask what a reasonable person could interpret differently. A good quote does not eliminate every uncertainty; it makes the important uncertainties visible before the work starts.
