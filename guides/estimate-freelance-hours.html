Project estimation

How to estimate freelance hours without underpricing the work

A practical method for turning an uncertain brief into a defensible hour range, a realistic quote, and a scope boundary you can explain.

Updated September 18, 2026 · 9 minute read

This is general business information, not legal, tax, or accounting advice. Estimates should reflect your actual delivery process, market, and contract terms.

An estimate is a range, not a promise

Underpricing usually begins before the quote. It begins when you treat an estimate as a single number produced from a vague brief. A client says “small website” or “quick campaign,” and your mind jumps to a familiar-looking total without checking the work hidden inside it.

A better estimate is a structured range. You define the outcome, break the work into smaller packages, estimate each one, add the work that always exists around the deliverable, and then calibrate the result against what similar projects actually consumed.

The goal is not perfect prediction. The goal is to avoid making a fixed-price promise based on the optimistic half of your imagination.

1. Define what must exist at the end

Write one sentence describing the finished outcome. Then list the deliverables and the exclusions. If you cannot describe what the client will receive, you cannot estimate the work with confidence.

For a website project, the definition might include five approved pages, a mobile layout, a contact form, analytics setup, one training call, and handover files. The exclusions might include copywriting, photography, paid advertising, advanced integrations, legal review, and ongoing maintenance.

Also record the dependencies. The estimate assumes that the client supplies brand assets by a certain date, appoints one decision-maker, and returns consolidated feedback within an agreed number of business days. A project with three separate approvers contains more coordination than the same project with one.

2. Break the project into work packages

Do not estimate the whole project in one line. Break it into activities small enough that you can imagine the work clearly. A useful structure is:

  • Discovery: kickoff, requirements, research, and technical checks.
  • Setup: accounts, files, environments, templates, and project organization.
  • Production: the main work that creates the deliverable.
  • Review: internal quality checks and client feedback rounds.
  • Revision: reasonable changes within the agreed scope.
  • Handover: export, documentation, training, and final approval.
  • Administration: proposals, scheduling, invoicing, and project management.

Each line should be concrete enough to challenge. “Design” is too broad. “Create two homepage directions, adapt the approved direction to five desktop page templates, and prepare the mobile layout” is estimable.

3. Estimate optimistic, likely, and pessimistic hours

For each work package, write three numbers:

  1. Optimistic: everything needed is available, feedback is clear, and no surprise appears.
  2. Likely: the normal amount of friction occurs.
  3. Pessimistic: the most reasonable difficult scenario occurs, not a disaster scenario.

A weighted estimate reduces the temptation to use only the optimistic number. A common formula is:

Weighted hours = (optimistic + 4 × likely + pessimistic) ÷ 6

This formula is not magic. It simply gives the likely case more influence while still recognizing uncertainty on both sides.

4. Include the work around the deliverable

Freelancers often underprice because they estimate the artifact and forget the system around it. The artifact may take twenty hours; the project may take thirty-five.

Do not leave these activities out:

  • Writing or clarifying the brief
  • Preparing files and organizing feedback
  • Status updates and project calls
  • Internal quality assurance
  • Waiting for and consolidating revisions
  • Exporting assets in the required formats
  • Explaining the finished work and handing it over
  • Answering questions after delivery

Some of this work may be included in the project fee rather than shown as a separate line, but it still consumes capacity and must appear in the internal estimate.

5. Calibrate with your actual project history

An estimate becomes more useful when you compare it with reality. For each completed project, record estimated production hours and actual hours. Then calculate your calibration factor:

Calibration factor = actual hours ÷ estimated hours

If three recent projects took 18, 22, and 26 hours against estimates of 15, 20, and 20, the factors are 1.2, 1.1, and 1.3. A simple average is 1.2. That does not prove every future project will take 20% longer, but ignoring the pattern makes underestimation predictable.

Use the factor only when the comparison is reasonable. A rushed logo project and a long website build have different uncertainty. Segment your history by service type, client type, and delivery process.

6. A worked example

Assume a client needs a five-page service website and will provide approved copy and brand assets. The work packages might look like this:

  • Discovery and technical setup5.3 h
  • Design and production24.7 h
  • Revision allowance6.5 h
  • Admin and handover4.2 h
  • Expected delivery time40.7 h

Add a 15% uncertainty and scope-risk allowance because the brief is clear but the client is new:

40.7 × 1.15 = 46.8 internal hours

Round upward to 48 hours rather than down to 45. If the internal rate is $95, the labor and risk component is about $4,560 before external costs, tax, or reserve. Use the project quote calculator to add those items and set the deposit.

The result does not mean the market will accept the price. It means the price was derived from the work you believe the project contains. If the client cannot pay it, reduce the scope or change the delivery method rather than pretending the hours disappeared.

7. Turn the estimate into a boundary

Your estimate should become part of the agreement, even if the client sees a fixed total instead of an hourly breakdown. State the assumptions that make the estimate valid.

For example:

“The project price includes five page templates, two rounds of consolidated revisions, one kickoff call, one review call, analytics setup, and one training session. It assumes the client supplies approved copy and brand assets before production begins and returns each review within five business days. Additional pages, new stakeholder directions, extra revision rounds, or integrations are handled through a written change order.”

Now the estimate has a boundary. If the request changes, the conversation is about the new requirement rather than whether you were “wrong.”

8. Increase the estimate when uncertainty is structural

Some uncertainty is normal. Some is a warning. Add more risk or a discovery phase when:

  • The brief is still changing during the proposal stage.
  • Multiple people can approve the work without a clear final decision-maker.
  • The project depends on an unfamiliar platform, integration, or API.
  • The client cannot supply examples of what “good” means.
  • The deadline requires leaving normal working hours or dropping other clients.
  • The client has a history of late feedback, unpaid invoices, or scope expansion.

If the uncertainty cannot be priced, define a paid discovery phase. It reduces risk for both sides and produces better information before the larger commitment.

9. Review the numbers before you send the quote

Run this checklist:

  1. Can I explain the outcome in one sentence?
  2. Have I listed every deliverable and exclusion?
  3. Did I include revisions, meetings, QA, and handover?
  4. Did I use a range instead of a single optimistic number?
  5. Did I compare the estimate with similar completed work?
  6. Does the price include risk, tax, costs, and a deposit?
  7. Is there a written change-order process?
  8. Can I deliver the scope at this price without sacrificing the rest of my business?

If any answer is no, the quote is not ready. Estimating is not a test of confidence. It is a control that protects the client relationship, your schedule, and the profit that keeps the business alive.
