Quote with a boundary

What belongs inside the project price

A useful quote is not only the time spent creating the final deliverable. It covers discovery, setup, communication, revision, quality control, file preparation, handover, and the commercial risk that the scope may change.

Included hours versus extra work

List what the price includes: number of concepts, revision rounds, meetings, formats, and delivery date assumptions. Anything outside that list becomes a new written request and, when appropriate, a change order.

A deposit is not a bonus. It funds the beginning of the work and filters out prospects who are not ready to commit.

A simple quote structure

  1. Outcome: what changes for the client after delivery.
  2. Deliverables: exactly what you will produce.
  3. Scope: included revisions, meetings, and formats.
  4. Timeline: dependencies and dates that rely on client feedback.
  5. Price and payment: deposit, milestones, due dates, and late-fee terms.
  6. Approval: a clear written acceptance path before work begins.

When the estimate is wrong

Do not hide a material estimation error by reducing your own rate to zero. Discuss a change order early, document the new requirement, and explain how it affects the delivery date or price. That is more professional than silently producing an unprofitable project.

Questions about fixed quotes

Should the client see my hourly rate?

Usually not necessary. The client buys the outcome and a defined scope. Your internal rate is still essential for deciding whether the fixed price is profitable.

How much risk buffer is enough?

There is no universal percentage. Use more when the brief is vague, the client is new, the technology is uncertain, or many stakeholders must approve the work.

What if the client asks for a discount?

Reduce scope, not the value of the same work. Remove deliverables, revision rounds, meetings, or speed. Protect the core margin.
