Understand the number

Why your rate is higher than a salary divided by 2,080

A full-time employee can be paid for vacation, training, management, and downtime. An independent professional usually can bill only for a portion of the working week. The rest goes to sales, proposals, administration, learning, invoicing, and finding the next client.

The formula

Required revenue = desired income + annual business costs. That amount is then divided by realistic annual billable hours. Finally, add a safety buffer so a slow month or underestimated project does not erase your profit.

The result is a floor for your pricing model, not a promise that every prospect will accept it. If the market will not pay it, change the offer, audience, or delivery model.

What to include in business costs

  • Accounting, legal, insurance, and banking fees
  • Software, hosting, hardware replacement, and professional development
  • Marketing, portfolio upkeep, networking, and sales costs
  • A tax reserve appropriate to your jurisdiction and business structure
  • An emergency fund for illness, delays, and seasonal demand

How to use the result in a quote

Keep the hourly rate as your internal cost of time. For fixed-price work, multiply it by every hour the project will consume, including revisions and project management. Add risk, tax, and a deposit as shown in the project quote calculator.

Questions about hourly rates

How many billable hours are realistic?

Solo service businesses often bill between 15 and 30 hours in a 40-hour week. Your number depends on lead generation, admin load, and whether you sell productized work.

Should I increase the rate every year?

Review it at least annually. Costs, demand, skills, and utilization change. Raising new-client rates first is usually easier than changing existing agreements.

Can I use a rounded rate?

Yes. Round upward to a defensible number. Do not round down below the calculated floor unless you deliberately accept lower profit for a strategic reason.
